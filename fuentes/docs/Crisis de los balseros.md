The Cuban Rafter Crisis . These were events that took place in Havana , Cuba in the summer of 1994 , motivated by the policy of the United States government to welcome to the mainland all Cubans who reached the American shores.

Background
In 1990 , with the decline of the Socialist Bloc and a year later with the disintegration of the Soviet Union , the Cuban economy was stifled and Cuban society entered what the Commander-in-Chief called the Special Period , a stage of deep economic depression, shortages of the main products of the basic food basket, means of transport and electric lighting.

These deficiencies led to discontent among the population of certain neighborhoods in Havana, such as in the Regla municipality , Valle Oculto or La Verdolaga and San Miguel del Padrón , such as Juanelo , Rosalía , Rocafort , Martín Pérez , La Fernanda, with individuals of striking behavior according to lovers of order, and public desertions were involved.

Young people found their options for recreation and well-being limited compared to the recently ended 1980s . The hardships brought on by material shortages, energy scarcity, food shortages, and transportation problems were the catalysts for a new wave or exodus to the peninsula located in the north of the Cuban archipelago.

From 1991 onward , illegal departures from the country increased. Between 1991 and 1994, the United States received 12,808 undocumented Cuban immigrants, while accepting only 3,794 applications for legal entry. In early 1992 , violent incidents occurred at the Tarará Naval Base , closely linked to the issue of illegal departures, resulting in the deaths of young military personnel stationed in the former pioneer town.

In the following months, events unfolded that resulted in the deaths of young people, the elderly, and people of all ages, races, and genders. In the municipality of Regla, in the spring of 1994, before the crisis erupted at the Havana docks, a street altercation also occurred, resulting in the death of a young man who lost his life while attempting to leave the country illegally.

Events
On August 5 , 1994, eight days before the maritime border was opened, another event occurred that spurred the Cuban government's decision. A serious riot broke out in a working-class neighborhood of Havana following an announcement by Radio Martí , a Miami- based radio station with a subversive slant, that ships were coming to Havana to round up anyone who gathered on the Malecón.

People gathered, but no ship ever reached the shore. It was all a hoax. The situation, already tense in Cuba due to the severe economic crisis, worsened when they realized the ships weren't coming and that they had been deceived. They rioted and began throwing stones at businesses, breaking windows, smashing property, and assaulting people, including police officers.

The pressure of illegal migration resulting from the economic crisis had reached its peak. The continued hijacking of boats, with loss of life in several cases, and the outbreak of public disturbances in Havana, led the Cuban government to decide on August 12 to eliminate restrictions on illegal departures.

It was then that Fidel Castro challenged Bill Clinton , then President of the United States, to accept all Cubans who wished to come. The magnitude of the crisis was such that the Cuban community residing in Miami issued a statement to Cuba to try to warn those who were taking the risk that leaving the island was not a good idea, in which it said the following:

"Please, do not go out to sea. It's total madness; there are currently about 200 rafts at sea. They're going to sink because the coast guard doesn't know what to do anymore."

In response to this situation, on August 19 , 1994, the Clinton administration reversed course on immigration policy toward Cuba and announced that rafters would be barred from entering U.S. territory. Breaking with a 35-year-old policy of exception, these migrants would be interned in facilities outside the United States.

Most Cubans intercepted by the U.S. Coast Guard in makeshift boats beyond the twelve-mile limit of Cuban territorial waters were taken to the mouth of Guantanamo Bay , occupied by the United States. More than 30,000 Cuban migrants ended up interned in U.S. military bases in Guantanamo and Panama .

Sent to Guantanamo
Between August and September 1994, a total of 32,362 Cuban citizens were intercepted at sea and transported to Guantanamo by U.S. Coast Guard vessels. The mass arrival necessitated the daily construction of 3,000 new tents, field hospitals, and drinking water systems. With the arrival of the Cubans, the total refugee population at the base swelled to nearly 50,000, as Haitians were also present.

New migration agreements
Ultimately, the Democratic administration shifted its focus to negotiations that led to the signing of new immigration agreements. This agreement was signed on September 9 , 1994. An understanding was reached to normalize the flow of legal immigration.

The United States government pledged to grant a minimum of 20,000 visas annually and provide other assistance to potential Cuban emigrants. The Cuban government assumed responsibility for controlling the flow of illegal migration from Cuban shores to the United States.
