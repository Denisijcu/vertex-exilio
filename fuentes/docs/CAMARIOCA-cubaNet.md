HAVANA, Cuba — In late September 1965, Fidel Castro surprised the world by publicly announcing that the port of Camarioca, north of Matanzas, would be opened to allow Cubans traveling from the United States to the island to pick up their families. The news immediately reached the United States, where it was met with suspicion; but two days later the dictator reiterated his intention to open this corridor and even send two daily flights from Havana to Miami, filled with those who wished to leave the island.

A few hours later, hundreds of boats docked in the small port. After years of separation and silence, the exiles returned to find their loved ones. On October 3, 1965, the maritime bridge between Camarioca and Florida was officially opened, marking Fidel Castro's first use of mass migration as a method to pressure the United States.

The then-president of the United States, Lyndon B. Johnson , responded to the provocation that same day, stating that the United States would welcome Cubans seeking asylum there. The first group of refugees arrived at Marathon Key on October 7.

A total of 2,979 emigrants arrived on U.S. soil during the 42 days the maritime corridor was open. On November 15, after both governments reached an agreement, the bridge was permanently closed. Migration via Camarioca was replaced by the so-called Freedom Flights , which continued until 1973. During those eight years, approximately 260,000 Cubans emigrated to the United States by air.

Camarioca defined a modus operandi for the communist regime. Fidel Castro would repeat the strategy in 1980, with the great exodus through the port of Mariel ; and in 1994 with the rafters' crisis.

Despite the propaganda deployed, each of these migratory chapters, added to the one currently taking place, has revealed the growing discontent and opposition of millions of Cubans to the political system that has punished the Island since 1959.
